CSRF via GET

Your browser will happily spend your money for a stranger. It just needs an image tag to ask.

01The Big Analogy

Head First Moment

Imagine you're a regular at your bank's restaurant-themed loyalty program — you're recognized by your loyalty card, which stays in your wallet at all times. Now imagine a completely unrelated restaurant across town has this policy: "anyone holding ANY loyalty card from ANY restaurant chain can charge food to whatever account that card belongs to." You'd never do that on purpose. But if your wallet is just sitting there and the card is scanned automatically the instant you walk in the door — without you presenting it, without you being asked — you've just paid for a stranger's dinner without ever knowing it happened.

Your bank session cookie is that loyalty card. Browsers attach cookies to a request based on which SITE the request is going TO — not which page you were looking at when the request fired. A completely unrelated website can make your browser send a request to your bank, cookie and all, without your bank ever finding out the request didn't come from you on purpose.

You, logged into the bank

Visit localhost:8000/. You get a session cookie. Balance: $1000. All normal so far.

You, browsing an unrelated site

Visit localhost:8001/ — "free cat pictures." A hidden image silently fires a request back to the bank. Balance: $900.

02The Attacker's Entire Payload

Here is the complete attack, in one line of HTML, from node/attacker/server.js:

<img src="http://localhost:8000/transfer?to=attacker&amount=100" style="display:none" alt="">

Nothing else on the attacker's page matters. The moment your browser loads this page — any page, anywhere, that contains this one tag — it fires a real GET request to localhost:8000. Compare that to the bank's vulnerable route:

if (path === '/transfer' && req.method === 'GET') {
  // THE VULNERABILITY: authenticated ONLY by whatever cookie arrived --
  // no check on WHERE the request came from, no proof of user intent
  balances[session] -= amount;
  balances[to] += amount;
}
Why this matters Notice what's completely absent from the bank's code: any check of the Referer or Origin header, any CSRF token, any confirmation step. The ONLY thing standing between "you" and "an attacker moving your money" is a cookie — and cookies don't know or care which page asked for them to be sent.
